# Conferences as Work: A Guide for Graduate Students

Academic conferences look like social events, and parts of them are. But for a graduate student, a conference is primarily a professional setting in which the people who will later review your papers, evaluate your job applications, write your recommendation letters, and collaborate with you are all in one building for a few days. Treating the conference as work rather than as a trip is the single habit that makes everything else in this guide easier. The fun is real, and you should have some, but it is a byproduct of doing the work well rather than the point of the trip.

Conferences are also part of the job market, in ways that are often invisible to students until the opportunity has passed. Postdoctoral positions, faculty searches, and industry hiring all run partly through conference conversations, and the timing of those conversations matters.

This guide begins with how to choose which conferences to attend and how they fit into your career timeline, and then works through three phases: what to do before you go, how to conduct yourself while you are there, and how to follow up afterward. A section near the end discusses how the same advice plays out differently depending on your temperament, because the effort that "seeming natural" requires is not the same for everyone.

## 1. Choosing conferences and planning for the job market

### 1.1 Which conferences, and how many

Travel money is limited, and not every conference is worth attending. Over the course of a PhD, most students should aim for a deliberate mix rather than whatever happens to be nearby or wherever the lab went last year. The main types, with notes on how their value differs by discipline:

- **The large society meeting in your field.** Broad, crowded, and expensive, but this is where the field assembles and where most informal hiring conversations happen. The people you most need to meet are far more likely to be there than at any other meeting, and far harder to reach: you will not bump into them by chance, and many other people will be competing for their time. Getting to them takes preparation, an introduction, or a scheduled meeting (see Sections 2.4 and 3.3). Attend at least once early, to learn the landscape and see who works on what, and then attend in the years when you are on the market. **In computer science and much of engineering,** the large meetings are also where the respected proceedings are: papers are peer reviewed, published with DOIs by a recognizable society or publisher, and counted as primary publications, so a paper here is worth more on a CV than a talk anywhere else. **In most of biology,** the same meeting accepts talks and posters from short abstracts, largely first come, first served, and produces nothing citable; the value is real-time visibility and the people. **In some engineering fields (and some others),** the society meeting is also an explicit hiring venue, with search-committee members in attendance, candidate events, and sometimes departments collecting CVs at a table for short, non-binding conversations.
- **Small, specialized meetings and workshops.** A few dozen to a few hundred people, all working on something close to your problem. It is easy to find people and easy to fall into conversation with them, because the senior people are less overwhelmed and you will see the same faces at every meal; the tradeoff is that there are fewer of them, and the specific people you need may not have come. **In proceedings-based fields,** workshops usually have no proceedings or a lightly reviewed one, so a paper here counts for less on a CV, but they are good venues for early-stage work and a natural fallback when a paper is not accepted at the main meeting. **In abstract-based fields,** a specialized meeting is often the most useful conference you will attend.
- **Interdisciplinary venues.** Mind opening, and a chance to meet the giants of other fields, whom you would otherwise never encounter. The audience for any one talk is small, the norms are mixed, and the people most relevant to your immediate job prospects are probably not there, so the returns are long term and the short-term opportunity cost is real. These are the meetings to attend on someone else's money: apply often for the fellowships, scholarships, and travel awards that subsidize attendance at such meetings, and go when one comes through. Check whether the proceedings, if any, are regarded by the departments you are aiming for; often they are not.
- **Regional and local meetings.** Cheap, low stakes, and good practice for presenting and for the social skills in the rest of this guide. The easiest place to find and talk to people at random, but with the fewest people worth finding. Rarely of any CV value in any field.

Whatever the meeting, do not treat it as a way to get to know the faculty at your own institution. Your advisor and other faculty you already know can introduce you to people, and you should ask them to, but they have their own agenda at a conference and it is not escorting their students around. Expect introductions, not company.

When deciding whether a particular conference is worth it, consider:

- Whether the people you most need to meet will be there. Look at last year's program and the list of invited speakers.
- Whether it has a proceedings, and if so whether it is peer reviewed before acceptance, published by a recognizable organization with a DOI per paper, and well regarded in your field. In a proceedings-based field, this is usually the deciding feature; in an abstract-based field, it rarely applies.
- Whether you have something to present, and in what form. Presenting is worth much more than attending, because it gives people a reason to approach you, and an accepted contribution is frequently a condition of eligibility for the student travel awards and society grants that pay for registration, travel, and lodging. Between a talk and a poster, do not assume the talk is better: a talk reaches the room for fifteen minutes, whereas a poster keeps you in one place for two hours with people who chose to walk over, which makes it much easier to talk with a wide range of people, including senior people who would never have queued after a talk. Choose the talk when you need the room or when the meeting's culture treats talks as the serious format.
- Whether it has hiring features you need this year: search-committee presence, career fairs, recruiter booths, candidate events (see Section 1.2).
- Cost, including whether travel awards, student registration rates, volunteer positions, or society student memberships can offset it.
- Your advisor's judgment. Ask which meetings they think matter for your goals and whether they will be there to introduce you.

Weighing these often means holding off on one meeting to wait for a better one later in the year, and you and your advisor should make that call together early rather than deadline by deadline. In proceedings-based fields, also decide before you submit where the paper goes if it is rejected (a later conference, a workshop, or a journal), because the deadlines cascade and a rejection with no plan can cost a year.

A rough pattern that works for many students: one big society meeting early, one or two specialized meetings in the middle years when you have results to discuss, and then whichever meetings your target employers attend in the final two years. Apply for every student travel award you are eligible for; they are less competitive than they look and the applications are short.

### 1.2 Conferences as part of the job market

Conferences double as interviews, whether or not anyone calls them that. This is true for all three of the main paths out of a PhD.

**Postdoctoral positions.** Most postdocs are filled through people faculty already know, and many are created for a specific person rather than advertised. When a faculty member receives a new grant and starts thinking about whom to hire, the students who come to mind are the ones who asked a sharp question after a talk, walked them through a poster clearly, or followed up with a short, sensible email the year before. Timing matters. A student who starts talking to potential hosts in the final months before graduating is usually too late, because the positions for that cycle have already been filled or promised. A student with more than about two years left who raises postdoc positions is usually too early, because nobody can plan hiring that far out. The window in which to make those conversations explicit is roughly the last one to two years of the program, and the conferences in that window deserve the most preparation.

**Faculty positions.** In fields where the society meeting is a hiring venue, being there the year before you apply, presenting, and having a coherent two-minute answer to "what will you do next" is part of the application. If departments are collecting CVs, drop one off and treat any conversation that follows as a professional conversation rather than an interview: nothing said there is binding on either side, and the purpose is to be recognized when your application arrives. Find out when hiring cycles in your field run and which meetings fall in the months before applications are due.

**Industry positions.** Many conferences, particularly in computing and engineering, have career fairs, recruiter booths, and sponsor receptions. If industry is a possibility for you, find out which companies are sponsoring before you go, look at what their attendees work on, bring a one-page résumé in addition to your academic CV, and treat booth conversations as first-round interviews rather than swag collection. Recruiters at technical conferences are often engineers and researchers from the group that is hiring, not human-resources staff, and they will remember a good technical conversation.

**How to raise it.** Coordinate with your advisor. An advisor who knows you are looking can seed conversations ("[Advisor] mentioned you are finishing next year") in a way that you cannot do for yourself, and can tell you which people are likely to have positions. When you raise it yourself, do it in person rather than by cold email, in the window described above, and phrase it as interest rather than a request: "I'll be finishing in about a year and am starting to think about postdocs; your group's work on X is one of the directions I'm most interested in." That gives the other person room to say "let's talk more" or to say nothing, without an awkward refusal. Do not ask whether they have a position in a first conversation, and do not ask by email after meeting once (see Section 5.4).

Before the window opens, do not raise jobs at all. It is never too early to make a good impression, and the goal of an early-career conference is simply to be remembered well, so that the later, more pointed conversation has something to build on.

## 2. Before the conference

### 2.1 Decide what you want out of it

Write down two or three concrete goals before you leave. Examples of goals that are concrete enough to act on:

- Get feedback on the open question in Chapter 3 from two people who work on related methods.
- Meet three people at institutions where you might want to do a postdoc.
- Find out whether anyone else is working on the same system you are, and if so, how far along they are.
- Present your poster well enough that at least a few people ask a question you have to think about.

"Network" and "see what's out there" are not goals; they are hopes. A concrete goal tells you which sessions to attend, which people to approach, and whether the trip was worth it.

### 2.2 Study the program

Read the full program once it is released, not just the session titles. For each day, mark:

- Talks and posters directly related to your work (attend all of these).
- Talks in adjacent areas that you want to learn about (attend most).
- Talks by people you specifically want to meet (attend, and sit where you can reach them afterward).

Then build a short list of people, ideally 5–10, whom you would most like to talk to. Most of them should be faculty and senior researchers who might have a postdoc, sit on a search committee, or otherwise matter to your next step, along with a few postdocs and senior students from those groups, who are easier to reach and can tell you how the group actually works. Other students do not need to be on the list; you will meet them organically at talks and events (see Section 3.10). A list made entirely of famous names is a list of people who will be busy, so include some mid-career people who are doing the work you care about.

### 2.3 Learn something about the people on your list

For each person on your short list, spend fifteen to thirty minutes on their recent work. You do not need to read everything; you need enough to ask one specific, informed question. Look at:

- Their two or three most recent papers or preprints, especially the abstracts and figures.
- What they are presenting at this conference.
- Their lab website, to understand what their group is about and who is in it.

Take a few notes, and bring them with you (on your phone is fine). The goal is not to be able to recite their CV; it is to be able to say something like "I read your paper on X, and I was curious whether the result held when Y," which is a very different opening from "I really admire your work."

Do the same for the presenters of the sessions you plan to attend. Knowing the context of a talk before you hear it lets you follow it and ask a good question afterward.

### 2.4 Consider reaching out ahead of time

For the two or three people you most want to talk to, a brief email a week or two before the conference is appropriate and often welcome. Senior people's conference schedules fill up quickly, and a message ahead of time makes it much more likely you will actually get their attention.

Keep the message short. A good pre-conference email:

- Is four to six sentences.
- Says who you are, who your advisor is, and what you work on in one sentence.
- Names the specific thing of theirs you want to discuss.
- Makes a low-pressure request: fifteen minutes over coffee, or simply "I hope to catch you after your talk."
- Offers specific times if you are proposing a meeting, but makes clear that you are flexible.

Example:

> Subject: Meeting at [Conference] – [your topic] and your recent [their topic] work
>
> Dear Dr. [Name],
>
> I am a third-year PhD student in [Advisor]'s group at [University], working on [one-phrase description]. I read your recent paper on [specific paper], and I have been thinking about whether [specific question or connection to your own work].
>
> I will be at [Conference] next week and would value fifteen minutes of your time if you have any to spare, perhaps over coffee on Tuesday or Wednesday. If your schedule is already full, I completely understand and will try to say hello after your session on [day].
>
> Best regards,
> [Your name]

Do not attach a paper unless they ask. Do not send a second message if they do not reply; they may still find you at the conference, and if they do not, you can introduce yourself in person and mention that you wrote. If your advisor knows the person, ask your advisor whether an introduction by email would be appropriate; a warm introduction usually gets a better response than a cold one.

Reach out to only a few people this way. Sending ten such messages is impractical for you and, if word gets around, looks indiscriminate.

### 2.5 Prepare your own material

You will be asked "what do you work on?" many times. Prepare, and practice out loud, three versions of your answer:

- **One sentence** (about ten seconds). What problem, what approach, why it matters. This is what you say when someone asks at a coffee break.
- **One paragraph** (thirty seconds to a minute). Adds the main result or the current state of the work. This is what you say when they say "tell me more."
- **A few minutes.** The version you would give at a whiteboard or while walking someone through your poster.

Tailor these to your audience. The one-sentence version for a specialist in your subfield should not be the same as the version for someone in a neighboring field. If your work spans fields, prepare the short version for each audience.

If you are presenting, rehearse until you can give the talk without notes and finish inside the time limit with a minute to spare. Practice in front of your lab and take their questions seriously. For a poster, prepare the two-minute walkthrough and stand at the poster during your assigned session; the poster is not the presentation, you are.

Prepare a list of questions you want answered. Some are questions for specific people; some are questions you can ask anyone in your area ("has anyone found a good way to deal with X?"). Having questions ready gives you something to say when you cannot think of anything.

### 2.6 Practical preparation

- Have a way to exchange contact information quickly: a business card, a QR code to your website, or at minimum your email address memorized and your phone ready.
- Make sure your website or profile page is current, because people you meet will look you up.
- Coordinate with your advisor about who they will introduce you to and which sessions they want you to attend.
- Know the conference's code of conduct and how to reach the organizers if something goes wrong.
- Plan for sleep. Conference days are long, and the useful conversations often happen in the evening. Being alert for those matters more than attending every 8 a.m. session.

## 3. During the conference

### 3.1 Where conversations happen

The easiest places to start a conversation, roughly in order of ease:

1. **Poster sessions.** The presenter is standing there specifically to talk to you. Walk up, read for a moment, and ask a question. This is the lowest-risk interaction at any conference.
2. **Immediately after a talk.** Approach the speaker, say one sentence about what interested you, and ask a question. Others will queue behind you; keep it to two or three minutes and offer to continue later if the conversation is going well.
3. **Coffee breaks and meals.** Approach someone standing alone or a small group with an open posture (a gap in the circle). Standing alone at a break is common and not a signal that someone wants to be left alone.
4. **Organized social events**, such as receptions, student mixers, and lab dinners your advisor takes you to.
5. **Hallway encounters.** Fine when you recognize someone from your list and they are not visibly heading somewhere.

Ask your advisor and senior lab mates to introduce you to people. Being introduced is far easier than introducing yourself, and it is part of an advisor's job. It is also most of what to expect from your advisor at a conference: they are there to see their own colleagues and do their own work, and an advisor who makes three good introductions and then disappears for the rest of the day has done their part.

### 3.2 How to open

A good opening is short, specific, and gives the other person an easy next line. Some forms that work:

- "I enjoyed your talk this morning. I was wondering about [specific point]."
- "I'm [name], I work with [advisor] on [topic]. I read your paper on [X] and had a question about [Y]."
- At a poster: "Can you walk me through this?" or "What was the hardest part of getting this to work?"
- To another student: "What are you presenting?" or "Which sessions have you liked so far?"

Avoid openings that require the other person to do all the work ("So, what do you do?") when you could have looked it up, and avoid openings that are really about you ("I've been working on something I think you'd find interesting").

### 3.3 Approach with confidence

At a large meeting, the people you need to meet will not find you, and waiting for a natural moment usually means the moment never comes. Approach them. Walk up after the talk, join the circle at the break, say your name, and ask your question. You are a colleague in training, not an intruder, and a polite, direct approach is what senior people expect from students who are serious. Assertive here means clear and unapologetic, not forceful: state what you want in a sentence, and then let them respond.

When a few minutes in a hallway will not be enough, ask for a meeting. A request for coffee or lunch is entirely reasonable in some cases, particularly in your final two years and with someone whose work is close to yours. Make the request in person if you can, or by a short message if you cannot. Either way, it should show that you know their work and why your interests align with it, and it should be concise enough that someone reading it between sessions on a phone will actually read it and be able to answer with a yes and a time. Two or three sentences is right: who you are, what you want to discuss and why it connects to what they do, and a proposed slot or two with an offer to be flexible. A request that asks for their time without saying why, or that takes three paragraphs to say why, will not get an answer, and that is not personal. If they decline or do not reply, thank them if there is an opening to do so, and move on to the next person on your list.

### 3.4 Talk about their work, specifically

The most reliable way to have a conversation that a senior researcher remembers is to ask a specific question about their work that shows you understood it. Generic interest is forgettable; a specific question is engaging, because it lets them think out loud about something they care about.

Specific means:

- Referring to a particular result, figure, assumption, or method rather than the paper as a whole.
- Asking about the decision behind something ("why did you choose X over Y?") rather than asking them to summarize.
- Connecting it to something else concrete, whether another paper, a system you know, or a limitation you have hit yourself.

Listen to the answer. Follow-up questions that build on what they just said are worth more than prepared questions read off a list.

### 3.5 Advocating for your own work

You should talk about your work at a conference. That is one of the reasons you are there. But there is a difference between describing your work when asked and steering every conversation toward it.

Guidelines:

- Wait to be asked, or wait for a natural connection. When someone asks what you do, give the short version and stop. If they ask a follow-up, give the longer version.
- When your work is relevant to what they just said, say so briefly ("we ran into the same problem with X; we ended up doing Y") and let them decide whether to pursue it.
- Do not pitch. Do not ask senior people to read your paper, look at your poster, or give you feedback during a first conversation unless they offer. You can invite them to your poster session in passing ("I'm presenting on Wednesday afternoon if you're around"), once.
- Do not oversell. Claiming that a preliminary result is a breakthrough, or that your method solves a problem the field has struggled with, will be noticed and remembered. Describe what you did and what you found, and let them draw conclusions. Understating slightly is safer than overstating.
- Do not disparage other people's work, including work by people who are not present. Conferences are small worlds, and the person you are talking to may be that work's author's collaborator, former student, or friend.

A useful ratio to aim for in a first conversation with someone senior is roughly two-thirds about them and their work and one-third about you. Later conversations, once they know you, can be more balanced.

### 3.6 Compliments and flattery

A single, specific compliment is fine and often a good opening ("your talk was the clearest explanation of X I've heard"). Repeated or vague praise ("I love your work," "you're a huge inspiration") is uncomfortable to receive, gives the person nothing to respond to, and signals that you are more interested in them as a name than in their ideas. Senior researchers get a lot of this and tend to discount people who do it.

If you admire someone's work, the best way to show it is to demonstrate that you have read it carefully.

### 3.7 Respecting time and boundaries

Senior researchers at a conference are usually trying to see many people in a short time. Reading and respecting that is one of the most important skills in this guide.

- **Keep first conversations short.** Two to five minutes after a talk; ten to fifteen minutes at a coffee break unless they clearly want to continue. It is better to end a conversation while it is going well than to let it run until they have to end it.
- **Watch for exit cues.** Glancing at a watch or phone, looking past you, shorter answers, shifting posture, "well, it was nice to meet you." When you see one, wrap up: "I should let you go. It was good to talk with you; may I follow up by email?" Do not make them find a way to leave.
- **Do not block someone who is trying to reach the speaker or leave the room.** If a line is forming, finish your question and step aside.
- **Do not join a conversation that is clearly private**: two people speaking quietly and closely, someone taking a phone call, or an obviously personal discussion. If you cannot tell, wait, or approach later.
- **Do not follow people.** Do not trail someone from session to session, wait outside a restroom, or appear at their hotel. If you missed them, there will be another break, and you can email afterward.
- **Meals and evenings are not automatically open.** If your advisor brings you to a dinner, that is an invitation. Inviting yourself to a group dinner you overheard being planned is not. Ask, once, and accept no gracefully.
- **Respect a "no" the first time**, whether it is to a meeting, a coffee, or a question about their unpublished work.
- **Physical space.** Keep a normal conversational distance. Do not touch people beyond a handshake. This applies to everyone, and it applies more strictly, not less, to people you are enthusiastic about meeting.

If a senior person cuts a conversation short, do not take it personally and do not try to recover it on the spot. A short, polite email afterward will do more than a second approach that afternoon.

### 3.8 Asking questions in sessions

Asking a question after a talk is a good way to be noticed by everyone in the room, including people not on your list. It is also a good way to be noticed for the wrong reasons.

- Ask one question. Do not ask a two-part question, and do not ask a follow-up unless the moderator invites it.
- Keep the preamble to a sentence. State your question; do not explain your own work first.
- Ask questions you actually want the answer to, not questions designed to show what you know.
- Do not use question time to challenge the speaker aggressively. If you think a result is wrong, ask a neutral question ("how sensitive is this to assumption X?") and, if needed, talk to them afterward.
- If you are the speaker, answer briefly, admit when you do not know, and offer to talk afterward when a question is taking too long.

### 3.9 Social events, meals, and alcohol

Social events are part of the work. The same rules apply as during the day, with a few additions.

- Go to them, even if you are tired. A surprising amount of collaboration begins at receptions.
- Sit or stand with people you do not know at least some of the time, rather than only with your lab.
- If you drink, drink less than you would with friends. One drink is a reasonable ceiling on a night when you are talking to people you may want to work with. Nobody has ever regretted being the soberest person at a conference reception, and many people have regretted the alternative. If you do not drink, nobody will care; hold a glass of something if it makes you more comfortable.
- The people around you are still the people who will review your papers. Stories told at 11 p.m. get repeated.
- Keep your phone use to a minimum while in conversation. Looking things up together is fine; scrolling is not.

### 3.10 Other students: colleagues, not the agenda

Conferences are where you get to know your peers, the other students you will keep seeing for the rest of your career. That is valuable over decades, and it is not what keeps you employed next year. Your time at a meeting should go mostly toward visibility with the people who may have postdoctoral positions or sit on faculty search committees, and a conference spent entirely in the company of other students, however pleasant, may be a conference wasted. You will get to know students organically, by watching their talks and through brief interactions at sessions and events, without making appointments for it.

Be similarly realistic about collaboration. People say conferences are for forming collaborations, and occasionally they are: a group with a dataset that did not pan out but fits an effort of yours is a real opportunity. But most papers are not born at conferences, academia is competitive, and lead authorship matters on a CV. Spending a lot of conference time scheduling chats with other students about possible joint projects is usually counterproductive, both because those projects rarely materialize and because it displaces the conversations that do affect your prospects.

None of this is a reason to treat other students badly. You will spend much of your time near them, and how you treat them matters as much as how you treat senior people.

- Introduce people to each other when you can. Connecting two people who should know each other is one of the most valuable things you can do at a conference, and it costs you nothing.
- Include people standing on the edge of a group.
- Ask other students about their work with the same seriousness you would give a senior person.
- Go to other students' posters, especially ones with nobody at them.
- Do not gossip about advisors, labs, or other students. It travels.

### 3.11 Keep notes

At the end of each day, write down whom you talked to, what you discussed, and anything you promised to send or follow up on. Include a detail or two that will help you remember the conversation. Two weeks later, you will not remember which of the twenty people you met said the useful thing about your Chapter 3 problem, and the follow-up email that says "we talked about X" is much stronger than the one that says "we met at the conference."

### 3.12 If something goes wrong

Conferences are professional settings, and the same standards of conduct apply as at your institution. If someone behaves inappropriately toward you, whether harassment, discrimination, or anything that makes you feel unsafe, you are not obligated to handle it alone or to handle it politely. Find the code-of-conduct contact, tell your advisor or another trusted senior person, or leave the situation. Your advisor would much rather hear about a problem during the conference than after it.

## 4. Calibrating to your temperament

The advice above describes what good conference behavior looks like from the outside. How much effort it takes to produce that behavior varies widely from person to person, and the adjustments you need to make depend on where you start.

### 4.1 If approaching people is hard for you

Many students, including introverted students and students who are neurodivergent, find that the unstructured social parts of a conference cost far more energy than the sessions do. This is common, and it does not mean you are bad at conferences. It means you need to do more of the work in advance and to budget your energy.

Things that help:

- **Prepare scripts.** Write down your openings, your one-sentence description of your work, and two or three questions for each person on your list. It is not cheating to read your notes before walking over to someone.
- **Set a small, explicit quota.** For example, two new conversations per day. When you hit it, you are done for the day and can attend sessions or rest without guilt. A quota you can meet is better than a vague intention to "talk to people more."
- **Use the structured venues.** Poster sessions and post-talk questions have a defined script and a defined end, which makes them much easier than receptions. Do most of your approaching there.
- **Ask for introductions.** Tell your advisor and lab mates explicitly that you find this hard and would like to be introduced to specific people. Most will be glad to do it.
- **Give yourself permission to leave.** Have an exit line ready ("I'm going to catch the next session; it was good to meet you") and use it when you have had enough. Ending a conversation early is not rude.
- **Schedule breaks.** Skip a session in the afternoon to sit somewhere quiet. Leave a reception after forty-five minutes. Nobody is counting.
- **Manage the environment.** If crowds or noise are difficult, receptions are the worst part of the conference and poster sessions are the second worst; do your important conversations at coffee breaks or by arranging a meeting somewhere quieter. Eat and sleep on a schedule even if others do not.
- **Do not interpret your own effort as failure.** A conversation that was hard for you to start looks, from the other side, like a normal conversation. The other person cannot see the effort.

If keeping a conversation going is the hard part, remember that asking a question and then listening is a complete strategy. Most people are happy to talk about their work, and you do not have to supply half the content.

### 4.2 If approaching people is easy for you

If you are naturally outgoing, the risks run the other way. Talking to people costs you little, which means you can easily talk too much, too long, and about yourself.

Things to watch for:

- **Talking more than listening.** Aim for less than half of the airtime in a conversation with a senior person. If you notice you have been talking for more than a minute without a question from them, stop and ask one.
- **Steering toward your own work.** Enthusiasm for your project is good, but check whether the last three things you said were about you.
- **Staying too long.** Because leaving a conversation feels unnecessary to you, you may miss the other person's cues. Set a mental timer and end conversations on your own initiative.
- **Filling silences.** A pause is often someone thinking about your question. Let it sit.
- **Being the loudest person in the group.** Volume and energy that feel normal to you can dominate a small circle and push quieter people out. Make room for others; ask the person who has not spoken what they think.
- **Treating the conference as a party.** The fun is real, but the impression you leave is professional. Keep evening behavior within the range you would be comfortable having described to your committee.

Your natural ease is an asset. Use it to include people, make introductions, and put nervous students at ease, rather than to hold the floor.

### 4.3 For everyone

Conferences are tiring for almost everyone, and the fatigue accumulates over several days. Plan for your energy to be lower on the last day than the first, and put your most important conversations early. Do not judge your performance by the standard of the most socially fluent person you can see; judge it by whether you met the goals you wrote down in Section 2.1.

## 5. After the conference

### 5.1 When to follow up

Send follow-up emails within a week of the conference ending, ideally within two or three days, while people still remember you. After two weeks, you are reintroducing yourself rather than following up.

If someone gave you a specific task (send me the paper, send me that reference), do that within a day or two.

### 5.2 Whom to follow up with

Follow up with anyone who:

- Asked you to send something.
- Explicitly said "email me" or gave you their card.
- Had a substantive conversation with you that you would like to continue.

Do not follow up with everyone you shook hands with. A person you spoke to for ninety seconds in a hallway does not need an email, and receiving one may be slightly puzzling.

### 5.3 What to write

A follow-up email should be short, specific, and easy to answer or easy to ignore. Aim for four to eight sentences.

Structure:

1. Remind them who you are and where you talked, in one sentence, with a detail that anchors the memory ("we talked after your Tuesday session about the sensitivity of your model to X").
2. Deliver whatever you promised, or say the one substantive thing you wanted to say.
3. If you are asking for something, ask for exactly one thing, and make it small: a reference, a short answer to a specific question, a fifteen-minute call at some point. Do not ask for feedback on a whole paper, a letter, a postdoc, or a collaboration in a first follow-up.
4. Close without pressure. Do not ask when they will reply.

Example:

> Subject: Following up from [Conference] – [topic]
>
> Dear Dr. [Name],
>
> It was good to meet you at [Conference] last week; I'm the student from [Advisor]'s group who asked after your talk about whether [specific point] would hold under [condition]. Thank you for taking the time to talk it through.
>
> You mentioned a paper by [Author] on [topic] that I should look at. I found it and it addresses exactly the issue I had been stuck on, so thank you for the pointer. As promised, I've attached the preprint of our [topic] work; there is no need to reply, but I would be glad to hear if any of it is useful to you.
>
> I hope the rest of the conference went well.
>
> Best regards,
> [Your name]
> [One line: program, institution, website]

Attach things only if they were requested or you said you would send them. Do not attach your CV.

### 5.4 What not to do

Most follow-up mistakes come from doing too much rather than too little.

- **Do not send more than one email.** If they do not reply within two or three weeks, the answer is "not now." You may write again with something new to say months later, or reintroduce yourself at the next conference. Do not send "just checking in" messages.
- **Do not connect on every platform at once.** Sending an email, a LinkedIn request, following them on every social network, and starring all their repositories in the same afternoon reads as fixation rather than interest. Email is sufficient. Add one professional platform if you like, later.
- **Do not tag or mention them publicly** ("great to meet @Name at the conference!") unless they did so first. Many people find this uncomfortable, and it makes your private conversation their public relations problem.
- **Do not contact their students or lab members to get to them.**
- **Do not ask for large things.** A first follow-up that asks for a postdoc, a letter, a collaboration, or detailed feedback on your thesis puts the person in the position of either doing a lot of work for a near stranger or refusing a student, and most will simply not reply. Build the relationship first.
- **Do not send long emails.** A long email from someone they met once tells them that replying will be expensive.
- **Do not overshare or over-apologize.** You do not need to explain how nervous you were, apologize for taking their time, or tell them how much the conversation meant. One "thank you" is enough.

The underlying principle is that the other person should always feel that they can end the interaction at any point without cost. Every message you send should make that easy.

### 5.5 Keep a record

Maintain a simple file or spreadsheet of people you have met at conferences: name, institution, where you met, what you discussed, and the date of your last contact. It will be invaluable when you are asking for letters, looking for postdocs, or trying to remember who said what two years ago. Update it after each conference while your notes are fresh.

### 5.6 Report back

Tell your advisor what you learned, whom you met, and what follow-ups you have planned. Advisors often know the people you met and can tell you which follow-ups are worth pursuing and how. Share useful things you learned with your lab; other students may benefit from a reference or contact you picked up.

## 6. Checklists

### Planning

- [ ] Meeting compared on its features: people, proceedings, what you can present, hiring features, cost, timing.
- [ ] Contribution type chosen (paper, talk, or poster) with the field's norms and funding eligibility in mind.
- [ ] Fallback venue identified in case of rejection or lost funding.
- [ ] Travel award or registration waiver applied for.
- [ ] Job-market timing checked against your remaining time in the program.
- [ ] Advisor consulted about which meetings matter and whether they will be there.

### Before

- [ ] Two or three concrete goals written down.
- [ ] Program read; sessions marked.
- [ ] Short list of 5–10 people, with notes on each.
- [ ] Pre-conference emails sent to two or three people (optional).
- [ ] One-sentence, one-paragraph, and few-minute descriptions of your work practiced aloud.
- [ ] Talk or poster rehearsed to time, in front of others.
- [ ] Questions prepared, both for specific people and general.
- [ ] Contact-sharing method ready; web presence current.
- [ ] Code of conduct read.
- [ ] Coordinated with advisor on introductions.

### During

- [ ] Attended all directly relevant sessions.
- [ ] Approached at least the people on the short list (or met the daily quota).
- [ ] Asked specific questions; listened to the answers.
- [ ] Kept first conversations short and ended them well.
- [ ] Went to social events; kept evening behavior professional.
- [ ] Introduced at least one pair of people to each other.
- [ ] Wrote notes each evening.

### After

- [ ] Delivered anything promised within two days.
- [ ] Follow-up emails sent within a week; one per person; short; one small ask at most.
- [ ] No second emails, tagging, or platform sprees.
- [ ] Contact record updated.
- [ ] Reported back to advisor and lab.

## 7. A closing note

Almost everyone reading this guide will, at some point, do something on the "do not" lists. Talking too long, sending a slightly too eager email, or asking a clumsy question is normal and is forgiven quickly, especially in students. The behaviors that damage a reputation are the repeated ones: the student who is always pitching, always following someone, always sending a second and third email. If you catch yourself doing one of those, stop, and do not spend energy apologizing for it; simply do better at the next break or the next conference. Your advisor, and most of the senior people you will meet, were graduate students at conferences once and remember what it was like.
